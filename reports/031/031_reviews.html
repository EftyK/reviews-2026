The title is clear. However, given its prominence, the concept of geo-alignment should be made more explicit and consistently addressed throughout the paper, not only in the title.

The introduction is clearly written and supported by concrete examples.

I would strongly suggest separating this section into two parts, such as Introduction and Background/Lit Review

It takes a long time and many conceptual explanations and references before the paper clearly reaches its main objectives and contributions. Making the motivation, relevance and research gap explicit earlier would help readers quickly understand why this work matters and what it adds.

Suggestions:

Be more direct in the opening section about objectives, research questions and contributions.

Move some of the conceptual and methodological background into a dedicated section.

Explicitly emphasize the notion of geo-alignment earlier and more consistently.


Results:

Several figures would benefit from higher resolution.

There are elements of interpretation and discussion interwoven throughout the results section. You may want to consider a combined Results and Discussion section, followed by a shorter final discussion, though this is ultimately a stylistic choice.

In the Alps subsection (around the sixth paragraph), the described pattern would be stronger if it were visualized or quantified, or more clearly linked to how LLMs actually operate.

The results section could be condensed overall, as there is some redundancy in the information presented.

Map presentation could be improved:

Legends should be proper legends, not screenshots.

Remove unnecessary decimal places in class labels.

For Figure 4, it would be helpful to clearly reference Montello et al. (2014) in the legend and explicitly state that the map on the right originates from that work.

In the Kashmir case, it is unclear whether the buffer size was sufficient to capture the models’ outputs. This deserves clarification.

The DeepSeek results are notably fragmented and blocky. It would be interesting to discuss whether this reflects something about the model architecture or training data.

Discussion:

The discussion clearly highlights a key dual issue:

Bias and digital divide effects in LLM outputs AND The influence of the intrinsic nature of different vague cognitive regions.

Although Kashmir, California and the Alps are all VCRs, they differ substantially in their socio-spatial, cultural, geopolitical, historical and environmental characteristics. These differences, such as geopolitical sensitivity, cultural prominence or ties to natural features, clearly affect model behavior and should be emphasized as an important insight of the study.

Conclusions

The relevance and contribution of the study are clear.

This is a paper with a novel topic and significant theoretical value. The authors have captured frontier issues at the intersection of GIScience and GeoAI, revealing the unevenness of LLMs in spatial cognition through quantitative means. The article combines the classic geographical concept of Vague Cognitive Regions (VCRs) with LLMs and introduces Geo-Alignment as an analytical framework, which is a very valuable entry point.
I am very satisfied with this paper, but I still want to offer some exploratory suggestions regarding some issues:

1.The research appears to use only English for prompting. However, except for Northern and Southern California, the official languages of the Alps, the Sahara region, and the Kashmir region are not English. By using only English questions, is it possible that the results obtained from the models are biased towards the English-speaking world's impression of these regions, rather than the true cognition of the regions themselves?
Also, I guess that the country who trained these LLM models might have some influence on the corresponding tasks in the text. I’ll give an example: The DeepSeek model is trained by China and may have undergone training with a large amount of Chinese corpus, and the Kashmir region happens to include a part of China. In China's official maps, the Aksai Chin region is clearly designated as within China's territory, and in Figure 5, the DeepSeek model happens to mark the Aksai Chin region as False. Is this because China does not consider Aksai Chin to be a disputed area? I guess there might be some correlation between them.
In addition, although the authors mentioned in the conclusion that future work could include multilingual prompting, I suggest analyzing this more deeply in the discussion section. If asked in the local language context, would DeepSeek or GPT-4o's representation of the Sahara or Kashmir change?
2.I noticed that in Figures 2, 5, and 6, the boundaries of the countries covered are not drawn. In fact, I think that apart from the California experiment, it might be necessary to add national boundary information to the result maps. This way, one might see more clearly which models tend not to classify which countries/regions into the corresponding VCRs.
3.The experiment set the Temperature to 0, which is conducive to experimental reproducibility, but for the exploration of vague regions, is a single prompt sufficient? For example, would asking "Sahara" vs. "The Sahara Desert" produce different boundaries? Although it is not necessary to redo the experiment, I would suggest adding a subsection or discussion to demonstrate the impact of slight changes in prompts on the stability of the results. If the results are highly sensitive to prompts, then the conclusion of uneven cognition might partly stem from the differences in prompts.
4.I have a concern: When LLMs are at Temperature 0, even with the same input every time, the output results might still be different due to floating-point arithmetic reasons. In fact, I am somewhat worried about the border areas of these disputed regions (such as the border between Northern and Southern California); if the LLM is asked the same question 10 times, will the LLM's output be exactly the same? If different, this might also affect the final experimental results to a certain extent.
5.The text mentions that Gemini-2.5-Flash resulted in invalid outputs due to Token limits, so max_tokens was increased, but this actually fails to meet the requirement of controlling variables. Does the partial reasoning generated by Gemini affect the final binary or numerical judgment? This may actually affect fairness to a certain extent.
6.I noticed that in Figure 2, the texture of the DeepSeek result map in the Binary row is somewhat different from GPT-4o and Gemini. Is this a problem produced during image output? The green color in the DeepSeek result map appears lighter.

In their fluidly written and methodologically carefully executed submission, authors detail a structured approach and case study to assess how well LLMs reproduce cognitive regions. For one of the cases authors also compare outputs from a previous empirical study with human participants.

This method paper will certainly be welcomed at AGILE for all the geoAI folks out there, who wish to reproduce anything related to GIScience now with LLMs.

There is nothing majorly wrong with this contribution for any geoAI person interested in applying LLMs to create geospatial patterns of any topic of interest for the geoAI research community.

For the rest of GIScience and thus for the AGILE conference community, interested in developing and advancing the science of GIScience, I find this submission entirely lacking any theoretical foundations and contextual embedding in the geographic of GIScience (including behavioral geography and spatial cognition) theories to EXPLAIN seen patterns. The augmentation to prior related work is minimal, e.g., see
https://doi.org/10.1080/13658816.2014.900178 and https://doi.org/10.1080/13658816.2016.1273357, included for one of the described case studies.

Interestingly, authors motivate their LLM study with the shortcomings of human subject experiments, i.e., https://doi.org/10.1080/13658816.2014.900178 and prior work on social media analytics, i.e., https://doi.org/10.1080/13658816.2016.1273357 while authors reproduce the same pattern with LLMs…
I would argue 1:0 for human-subjects research, as motivated in the current paper!

This paper is reminiscent of the times when social media, simply because it was available, i.e., Twitter and Co., were mined to reproduce patterns of past GIScience research. Now, Twitter and Co. are being replaced by LLM and Co. As presented in this paper, while we can run much more data with greater speed and less effort, we have not learnt anything about the process that generated the found patterns of that prior GIScience research that is reproduced.

I have not been able to detect anything in the results that has augmented the state of the art of what is known about cognitive regions or why we need to do this research now with LLMs and not by running empirical studies, driven by hypotheses that emerge from prior scientific endeavors. What is the state of the art on cognitive regions? What is the cognitive process that generates all the patterns in the Figures? Who else has worked on this that can help explain the found patterns? Where is that spatial cognition research, where is that geography research that explains the pixel distribution in the “Sahara” and in the “Kashmir” regions? The current paper is devoid of any references to spatial cognition and (human) geography research, except for Montello and colleagues, who provided the “ground truth” of behavioral data.

All references used in this paper relate to the methods or to papers that also use LLM for something. From the result sections onwards, there are no references that relate to explain, discuss, interpret, and thus augment the state-of-the-art on cognitive regions. The entire paper rests on “intuitions” and interpretations of patterns, without any theoretical contextualization.

Armed with LLM-related methods, the output of https://doi.org/10.1080/13658816.2014.900178 is reproduced, all the while the paper motivates this kind of ( tedious, small-scale, etc.) behavioral research as a limitation that LLMs can overcome.
