6: strong accept
The paper presents a conceptually strong and original approach to human–wildlife encounter detection by introducing human disturbance areas (HDA), which better reflects ecological reality than proximity-only methods. It is very well written, clear and well formatted. Also, DASA section is well done.
The methodology is clearly formalized, with precise definitions, a transparent workflow, and a thorough sensitivity analysis that improves interpretability and reproducibility. The case study uses a large, heterogeneous real-world dataset and includes a meaningful comparison with ORTEGA, convincingly demonstrating the limitations of existing approaches. However, one of the shortcomings of the study is that it lacks ground truth validation; conclusions rely primarily on parameter sensitivity and plausibility rather than independent ecological validation of detected encounters.
I suggest clarifying that direct ground truth for human–wildlife encounters is rarely observable, and position the work as a methodological contribution rather than a validation study.

Also, the results are highly sensitive to parameter choices, particularly HDA_radius, which may limit the generalizability of the method across species, environments, or activity types.
It would be good to add in the discussion that HDA_radius is explicitly intended to be context- and species-specific, not a universal constant, and that the method is designed to support exploratory scenario analysis. You can also stress that other parameters show low sensitivity, indicating robustness of the overall framework.
The current title may lead readers to expect a generic encounter-detection algorithm, while the paper actually contributes a conceptual reframing of what constitutes an encounter. I recommend considering slightly more explicit reference to disturbance or human impact could better signal the paper’s originality and ecological relevance.

The paper “A New Spatio-Temporal Method for Detecting Human-Wildlife Encounters from GNSS Trajectories” presents a novel method for identifying areas in which trajectories from humans and animals suggest that the two species likely have crossed paths, potentially disturbing the latter. Understanding this dynamic is a relevant topic and of interest for the AGILE community.

The paper is well-written and presents methodology, experiments, and results in a clear way. The authors succeed in demonstrating that their method outperforms the baseline ORTEGA for the use case that is analyzed, underlining the relevance of the new method.

An aspect in which the authors may improve their submission concerns the transferability of the method. The paper frames it as a “human-wildlife encounter detection method”, omitting to express in critical parts of the paper (introduction and conclusion) that central aspects of the method will not transfer well to just any kind of animal. This is, in my opinion, not a weakness of the method, but a fact that should be highlighted in the publication.

For example, the assumption that “if an animal was recorded in April 1 and a human passed the same area at the same hour on April 2, it is reasonable to think an untracked animal was present on April 2” works well for the strongly site fidelitous chamois, yet not for nomadic species or species with large territories. Similarly, the visibility constraint depicted in Figure 7 is reasonable for a fairly large species in mountainous territory, but would not work well for smaller animals or species that rely more on their sense of hearing or smell.

Further minor comments:
- place 3.3.1 in section 4?
- Table 3: are these numbers averages?
- 4.4: “A 250 meter [...] radius was selected to be slightly higher than the average distance that chamois move away from the trails during the day”: unclear; is this the average distance between animals and trails? Or is it the average distance they flee when they are disturbed?

I recommend accepting the paper after minor revision.

This paper presents a method for detecting human–wildlife encounters using GNSS trajectories, introducing the concept of human disturbance areas to overcome the limitations of proximity-based approaches. The article is well written, and the method is clearly defined, implemented, and supported by a sensitivity analysis. While the authors acknowledge the lack of ground truth for validation, some aspects would benefit from further justification and explanation, particularly regarding the large discrepancy between the number of real encounters (185) and potential encounters (over 63k). The ecological relevance of potential encounters could be discussed more clearly. Overall, the article presents an interesting contribution and is well suited for the conference.
